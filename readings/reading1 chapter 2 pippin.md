Pong is a great example because of its simplicity. It’s stripped down, maybe barebone but very efficient at what it aims to do: The simplest iteration of a skill based competitive where players are on equal grounds.  Pippin shows that with this simple recipe for a game, there can be countless iterations of that game with changes and tweaks that can either work on the base rules to enhance them or to completely flip things around by replacing old rules with new ones. 

With these new versions of the pong game comes also new feelings to be evoked. Pippin argues that new rules not only affect the structure of the game, they also change drastically the feelings a player may or may not feel. A player may feel joy, anger, frustration, fear and many more. 

I was particularly interested in the unfair pong variation. At first glance you may think that it is just an unequal version of the game that induces frustration for one and gloat for the other. But if you look more closer new rules can start to appear. The player with the smaller bar may feel like he is winning more than the player with the bigger bar even though his score increases by one just like every time the fortunate player scores. Pippin makes a great analogy where he references it to david and goliath. My addition to the analogies is the myth of sysiphus because I feel like some player may like the hardest position and appreciate a constant unfair and harder gameplay. 


The vienese pong game is a humourous approach where the new rule added in this game asks you if you are playing this specific pong game in Vienna and will prevent you from playing if you are not in this city right now. Here the rules are not bound by the code. They are more tied to our social contract of telling the truth. There is no system written in the code that will check if you are really in Vienna. The game expects you not to lie. This shows that games can benefit from our emotional approach by enhancing the interaction with the game by making it more human.
